Methodology, Playing Levels & Progression

Who to play padel with to improve: the 6-3-1 rule

By Manu Carrilero · Sports Science Graduate (BSc CAFYD) & Padel Coach in Valencia

There is one question I get almost every week, and it has nothing to do with grips or with the bandeja: «Manu, what level should I be playing at?». Most people believe progress depends on lessons, on the racket or on the ball machine. It doesn't: it depends, above all, on who you play your matches against. The level of your opponents is the variable that shapes your progression the most, and yet almost nobody trains it.

I see it every weekend. Players who have been taking flawless lessons for three years and are still exactly where they were, because they only play comfortable matches afterwards. And players with far from polished technique who shoot up fast, because they have surrounded themselves with people who demand more. The court gives you back what you ask of it; if you always ask for the same thing, you always get the same thing.

Why the level of your opponents decides how much you learn

There are three very recognisable profiles in amateur padel. The one who only plays against people at exactly their level, living in a comfort zone where nothing new ever happens. The one who always plays downwards, who wins almost everything and goes home happy, but whose padel has frozen. And the one who always plays upwards, collecting new stimuli at a speed they cannot digest.

None of the three is a disaster, but all three share the same problem: monotony of stimuli. In motor learning we talk about the zone of proximal development or the optimal challenge point, and it says something very simple: you learn when the task is one step above what you already master. Not two steps, and not on the same step. One.

When the ball arrives too slowly and too predictably, your body has no reason to adapt: it repeats. When it arrives so fast that you can barely lift the racket, your body goes into survival mode and learns nothing, it just survives. Learning happens in that middle band where you have to work for it, but you still have time to decide.

And here is the key almost nobody understands: every level teaches you something different. Playing at your own level is not playing worse matches, it is the place where you can actually apply what you train. Playing above your level is not only about receiving a faster ball, it is about seeing how a point is built. Playing below your level is not a waste of time, it is the hardest exam there is: winning without being aggressive.

The 6-3-1 rule: split your ten monthly matches

I always give my students the same guideline, and it works from beginners to advanced players. Take your ten matches of the month and split them like this:

6 Your level

The laboratory where you apply what you learn.

3 Above your level

The exam of speed, reading and strategy.

1 Below your level

The exam of control: win without aggression.

Those numbers are a starting point, not a law. If you are just starting and struggle with frustration, you can stay at five matches at your level, two against stronger players and one downwards. If you have been playing for years and want a leap, push it to three or four against better players. What never changes is the structure: most matches in your productive comfort zone, a demanding minority and an easy minority.

Six matches at your level: the laboratory

These are the matches where learning actually consolidates. Against players of your level you have time to think, situations repeat and you can afford to miss without the match slipping away in three points. This is where you test the wall exit you have just been corrected on, where you decide to move up to the net at the right moment and where you check whether the bandeja you train survives the pressure of a real match.

The usual mistake is thinking that «playing with your own kind» is wasting time. It isn't: it is the only place where a new movement goes from the lesson to competition. Without these matches, what you learn in the session stays in the session.

Three matches against stronger players: the ball speed exam

This is where your level goes up, but not only because of speed. It is true that against better players the ball comes faster and with more spin, forcing you to prepare earlier, turn your shoulders better and shorten your swing. But there is a detail that surprises many people: sometimes the ball arrives slower and still hurts you much more.

That is pure strategy. A good player does not need to hit hard to win the point: they place the chiquita where you cannot reach it, they change the rhythm, they force you to move up with an uncomfortable ball, they wait at the net with the racket ready. Playing against them teaches you to read the point, to understand when to move up and when to drop back, and to pick up patterns you had never seen because nobody had ever shown them to you.

«From a lesson you take away a movement. From a match against someone better you take away an idea. And ideas are what change the way you play.» — Manu Carrilero, BSc CAFYD

One match against weaker players: the control exam

This is the hardest one and the least practised. Against weaker players the challenge is not to win: it is to win without being aggressive. You force yourself to give up your best weapon, to build the point with patience, to place instead of accelerate and to defend every ball as if it were match point.

It sounds easy and it isn't. Five minutes without being able to smash and you start to feel the temptation. Controlling it trains your head better than any motivational talk.

Two ways of learning padel: my story and my partner's

This is not textbook theory. I have lived it myself and I am watching it happen at home.

2005: me, with no references and a lot of hours

I started playing padel around 2005, and I learned from what I saw: the parents of a friend who played, and a friend. Not much else. There were no academies, no proper technical videos and, above all, no reference at all of what playing padel well looked like, because back then there were not many around.

Almost everything I played was against people at my level and slightly above. That made me improve, of course it did, but it cost me far more than it needed to. I needed four or five years to reach a level that, in a different environment, would have come much sooner. And I wasn't starting from zero: I came from tennis, table tennis, badminton and fronton.

Her: a whole family playing padel

My partner has had what I was missing. In her family padel is part of everyday life: her father plays at a 3.5 level in Valencia, her brother is around 4 and a bit, and her cousin and her uncle play too. She is surrounded by people who have been on court for years and play well.

On top of that, I coach her whenever we have a gap and we play together from time to time. And something happens there that money cannot buy: she doesn't just get the odd instruction during a match. She watches me play, she watches us play with her family, and from that she understands the structure of the point: when to move up, when to drop back, which shot works in each situation, why the ball goes there and not to the other side.

What you save when you are exposed to good padel with guidance

The result is clear. Where I needed four or five years to reach a level X, she has reached that same level in far less time, and starting from zero with a racket. No tennis background, no other racket sport. I arrived with more tools and still took longer.

The difference is not talent. It is the environment. She has been regularly exposed to much stronger players, but nobody threw her into the deep end: she plays accompanied, with her family and with me, at a level gap she can handle and with someone correcting her in the moment. That is exactly the line that separates being exposed to good padel from collecting beatings.

If you are not lucky enough to have a whole family playing, you build it: a stable group, a coach who corrects you and two or three better players who adopt you from time to time. And if you want to speed it up even more, record your matches. I had to learn by filming myself when almost nobody did; today I tell that story in my article on video analysis and recording yourself playing, and it is still the best-value shortcut I know.

How I hold myself back when I play with weaker players

This is the point I care about most and the one clubs handle worst. When I play with my partner as my doubles partner and we go to a friendly match against people at her level or slightly above, I set my own rules. They are not rules to show off: they are rules so the other side gets to play too.

  • I don't smash. Not one. It doesn't matter if the ball sits up perfectly and the gap is right there.
  • I defend at one hundred percent. In defence I hold nothing back: I come out of the wall, I dig out the low ones and I reach everything I can reach.
  • I attack at fifty percent. Placed bandeja, rulo, the odd tighter vibora, but never hunting for the three or the four.
  • I prioritise the ball that comes back. A long rally beats a pretty winner.

What do I get out of this? Three things at once. First, the other side has a good experience and leaves wanting to play again. Second, my partner plays a match where she can touch the ball and learn, instead of watching me finish points. And third, the most selfish one: I train placement, patience and control under pressure, which are exactly the things you never train when all you do is smash.

What really makes padel fun: getting the ball back

There is an idea I repeat often and everyone ends up agreeing with: the best thing about padel is not winning the point, it is making the point last. We enjoy it when the other side returns the ball, when there is a rally, when the game goes back and forth. A two-shot point bores anyone; a fifteen-ball rally hooks you even if you lose it.

That is why people enjoy playing against someone better: because they usually get a lot of balls back. What they enjoy far less is being run over. The difference between the two situations is not the level of the opponent, it is their attitude. The good player who adapts when playing down creates fans; the one who uses it to hold a smash festival creates ex-players.

Best of the worst, or worst of the best?

There is no right answer here, there is an honest self-assessment. Some people enjoy being the best of the worst: they like winning, controlling the match and feeling competent. Others prefer being the worst of the best: they accept losing almost everything in exchange for learning and rubbing shoulders with a level above them.

Neither stance is better than the other. What is a problem is not knowing which one is yours. If you need to win in order to enjoy yourself and you keep signing up for matches where you get swept aside, you will end up hating padel within three months. And if you handle beatings well but only ever play comfortable matches, you will be exactly the same player in five years.

My advice is to ask yourself one very specific question after every match: what am I taking away from this?. If the answer is «nothing», that match doesn't count as training, it counts as couch time with a racket.

The limit: controlled frustration, not daily humiliation

There is a sentence I use a lot with my students: a little well-controlled frustration is a gift. It is what pushes you to look for solutions, to change the plan, to improve your placement. Without that touch of discomfort there is no adaptation, not in padel and not anywhere else.

But the dose matters, a lot. Being exposed to frustrating situations every day is not training: it is wearing yourself down. The player who leaves the court angry every week ends up quitting padel, and not for lack of talent, but for lack of enjoyment. Frustration should be a guest who visits now and then, not someone who lives in your house.

My thermometer is simple. If after the match you can name one thing you learned or one situation you now understand better, the match was worth it even if you lost 6-1. If you leave without knowing what happened and with the feeling of barely touching the ball, it is time to recalibrate the level of your next opponents.

How we work on this in my padel lessons in Valencia and Paterna

In my padel lessons in Valencia and Paterna we don't just train shots: we also design who you play against. On the indoor courts of Elite 22 (Paterna) we combine technical sessions, ball machine repetition and controlled matches where the goal is not to win but to complete a specific task.

That way the student understands that the lesson and the match are the same thing. In our small group and couple formats it is easy to adjust the level of the opponents and set handicaps: one player cannot smash, the other can only play cross-court, the point starts with a high ball. If the match leaves you sore, the body comes first

Playing three matches a week against stronger players demands much more than it looks: more changes of pace, more accelerations and more load on the lower body. If you are accumulating elbow, shoulder or lower back niggles, the problem may be physical preparation rather than technique.

Frequently Asked Questions about who to play padel with

Is it bad to always play against players better than me?
It is not bad, but it is not enough. Always playing above your level gives you new stimuli, but leaves you no time to consolidate what you learn or to test your own shots. Ideally most of your matches should be at your level, with two or three per month against stronger players and one or two against weaker ones.
How many matches a month should I play against weaker players?
One or two per month, always with a clear goal: win without being aggressive. In those matches you force yourself to place the ball, to defend at one hundred percent and to give up the smash. You train control, patience and decision making, and you also let the other side enjoy returning balls.
What if nobody at my club is better than me?
Then you create the challenge yourself: handicap matches, internal leagues, Americano formats, or lessons and ball machine sessions so you receive a faster ball than usual. You can also agree rules with players of your own level that raise the demand, such as no smashing or a forbidden zone.

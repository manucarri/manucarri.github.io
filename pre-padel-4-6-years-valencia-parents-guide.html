Pre-Padel (4-6 years): what we teach, why, and how to choose the right racket

By Manu Carrilero · Sports Science Graduate (BSc CAFYD) & Padel Coach in Valencia

If your child is between 4 and 6 and you are thinking about signing them up for padel, it is normal to have doubts. Are they too young? Should we wait until 8? Do we buy a racket now or later? And above all: what exactly happens during 45 minutes on a court?

Here it is in one sentence: at this age we do not teach padel. We build the body and brain that will play padel later.

In this article I explain what we work on in the Pre-Padel group, why we do it this way —with the studies that support it— and how to choose a racket without wasting money or overloading your child's wrist.

Quick answer for parents in a hurry

  • Age: 4 to 6. No previous experience or own equipment needed.
  • Goal: physical literacy (balance, bouncing, throwing, catching, moving), not stroke technique.
  • Format: one 45-minute session per week, 5-8 children per court, rotating stations.
  • Equipment: a racket around 43 cm and 200-250 g, a soft ball or a balloon. The ball matters more than the racket.
  • Fee: 45 €/month, 0 € registration, 100% indoor covered court.
  • Good sign: they leave happy and want to come back next week.

1. What Pre-Padel is and what it is not

It is not an adult class in miniature

A 5-year-old is not a scaled-down adult. They are about 110 cm tall, weigh around 20 kg, and their nervous system is still under construction. Their sustained attention lasts about 5-8 minutes per task. Asking them to repeat a technical stroke for twenty minutes is not demanding: it is pointless.

That is why Pre-Padel has no queues, no scoreboard and no five-point corrections. It has stations, games and short challenges. The session looks more like a movement-skills circuit with a racket at the end than a technique class.

What physical literacy means

Physical literacy is the set of basic skills that let a child move confidently in any sport. They fall into three families:

  • Locomotion: running, jumping, turning, braking, crawling.
  • Object control: throwing, catching, bouncing, striking.
  • Stability: static and dynamic balance, postural control, landing.

These skills do not appear on their own. The research is clear: they need teaching, practice, feedback and repetition (Logan et al., 2012; Morgan et al., 2013). A child who reaches age 9 without a jumping or catching pattern is not "untalented": they are a child with an exposure gap.

2. Why, at 4-6, we train the body before the stroke

What the science says about this age

Between the ages of 3 and 8, the brain and nervous system grow at a rate that will not return. This is when basic movement patterns are built, and most children have the potential to master them by around 6-8 (Gallahue et al., 2012; ACT Government, 2022).

Let us be honest: the "windows" of the classic athlete-development model are debated. Van Hooren and De Ste Croix (2020) reviewed the evidence and concluded that there is not enough support for generic windows per ability. What does hold up is simpler and more useful: the more movement variety at this stage, the better the base for everything that follows.

That is why we do not tell families "use it before it closes". We say: this stage gives the best return on building a movement repertoire. It does not expire at 7; it simply costs more later.

The virtuous circle: competent → enjoys it → keeps playing

Motor competence and physical activity feed each other. A child who moves well plays more, enjoys it more and keeps practising; one who feels clumsy compares themselves, gets frustrated and drops out (Lubans et al., 2010; Barnett et al., 2016).

What we do at 5 does not only improve their future padel: it influences whether they will be an active adult at 40. That is the real return of this stage.

What we do NOT do (and why)

  • We do not specialise. Early specialisation is linked to more overuse injuries (Carder et al., 2020) and more burnout (Giusti et al., 2020), without improving sporting results. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends delaying specialisation until late adolescence (AAP, 2016).
  • We do not use adult equipment. A heavy racket or a standard ball forces the child to compensate with the wrist and steals the time they need to strike.
  • We do not correct five things at once. One correction per session, and better as an image ("the racket looks at the sky") than as a technical concept.
  • We do not keep score. At this age the goal is taking part, not winning.
  • We do not compare children. Each has their own maturation rhythm, and at 5 the normal range is enormous.

3. The five blocks we work on court

Every session touches the five blocks, in a different order and with different games. They are not "subjects": they are the pieces of movement that later combine into a stroke.

3.1 Balance and postural control

Every stroke starts and ends on a foot. We work on static balance (on one leg, eyes closed), dynamic balance (walking a line, going over obstacles) and landing control. A child who learns to land well at 5 has a lower injury risk at 13.

3.2 Bouncing, throwing and catching

The ball is the best physics teacher there is. Bouncing, throwing and catching teach the child to read trajectories, calculate timing and adjust force. It is the direct prerequisite of the point of impact: a child who cannot catch with their hands will struggle to know where to place the racket.

3.3 Moving and changing direction

Running, braking, turning and starting again. Without this there is no padel: most of the game is about getting to the ball well. We use cones, hoops, floor ladders and tag games.

3.4 Rhythm and timing

Rhythm is the base of timing. Clapping, songs, rhythmic jumping and balloons that cannot touch the floor. A child with good rhythm learns earlier to strike at the right moment, because they already know when things happen.

3.5 First contact with the racket

This is where padel begins, but without technical demands. Foam racket, balloon or soft ball, free hitting. The goal is for the racket to be a friendly object, not a precision tool. Fine technique will come at 7-8, when implement control allows it.

4. Concrete exercises you will see in a session

These are some of the ones I use most, with what each one trains and why it works:

Exercise What it trains Why it works
The island Lateral movement and balance Forces them to move without crossing their feet, the pattern padel later uses to cover the court.
The balloon that will not fall Point of impact and rhythm The balloon floats and gives time. The child strikes many times without fear of missing.
Bounce, bounce, racket Hand-eye coordination A natural progression: first the hand, then the racket. No steps skipped.
Throw and catch Catching and trajectory reading It is the base of ball reading, with the hands before the racket.
The 4-station circuit Movement variety and autonomy Everyone is active all the time. No queues, no waiting.
Shadows and mirrors Imitation and laterality At this age children learn by copying. The visual model is worth more than the explanation.
Tag with braking Change of direction and deceleration Trains deceleration, where foot and knee injuries most often happen.
Over the rope First strike and the idea of a net Introduces "sending it to the other side" without complex rules or a scoreboard.

None of these exercises is "padel" in the strict sense. All of them are padel in the deeper sense: they build the pieces the stroke will need in two or three years.

5. What a 45-minute session looks like

The structure is almost always the same, because routine provides security and lets the child anticipate what comes next:

  • 0-5 minutes · Playful warm-up. An activation game with a ball or balloon. It helps them leave the backpack behind, warm up and connect with the group.
  • 5-35 minutes · Stations. Four or five stations of 5-6 minutes: balance, bouncing, throwing, moving and racket. Everyone rotates through all of them.
  • 35-45 minutes · Cooperative final game. A game that brings together what was worked on, with no scoreboard. It closes with a question ("what did you get right today?") so the child takes away a concrete win.

Why 45 minutes and not an hour? Because a child's sustained attention runs out before an adult's. In three quarters of an hour, intensity stays high from start to finish, without the last ten minutes of fatigue and distraction that ruin motivation. Adults train for 60; children for 45.

Why 5-8 children per court? Because it allows individual attention without losing the social play that, at this age, is part of learning.

6. The evidence behind every decision

Why stations and not queues

A queue is dead time. In a queue of eight children, each one strikes once every eight turns. With stations, everyone is active almost all the time. Farrow and Reid (2010) showed that when the court is reduced and a slower ball is used, children strike more times per session and learn more. Practice density is not a luxury: it is the factor most closely related to learning.

Why balloons and slow balls

A balloon floats, gives time and forgives. A low-compression ball does the same, but more realistically. Buszard and colleagues (2014) found that, with 6-9 year olds, the combination of a red ball and a small racket improved stroke technique and accuracy, and that the ball had more influence than the racket.

In 2016, the same group's systematic review —25 studies and 989 children— confirmed that adapted equipment improves performance in 18 of the 25 studies, that children prefer it, and that the effect is greater in the least skilled. Translated to the court: before buying the perfect racket, slow the ball down.

Why we do not correct the grip to the millimetre

At 5, the goal is for the child to pick up the racket with enthusiasm. Fine grip correction comes at 7-8, when they already control the implement and can understand an instruction. Correcting earlier creates tension, not learning.

There is another reason: adapted equipment favours implicit learning, that is, learning by moving without thinking about every detail (Buszard et al., 2014b). Too much early verbal instruction breaks that process and makes the child think about their arm instead of the ball.

Why there is no scoreboard at this age

Because the goal is participation, not results. A scoreboard introduces comparison, fear of error and, in many cases, drop-out. When competition arrives, it will arrive with adapted rules and no harsh elimination. Before 9-10, competing means playing.

7. How to choose your child's racket

This is the question I get most on WhatsApp. The short answer is: weight, shape and grip, in that order. Length matters less than it seems, because the rules limit it heavily.

7.1 Weight comes first

In padel, a racket cannot be longer than 45.5 cm (FIP). That is why junior rackets run from 43 to 45.5 cm: they barely change in length. What really changes is weight, which can range from 200 to 350 grams. That is where the decision lies. On the market, most rackets for ages 4-6 weigh around 230-265 grams: always look at the lower end of that range.

Age Approx. height Length Weight Shape and core
4-6 years 100-115 cm ~43 cm 200-250 g Round · foam or soft EVA
7-8 years 115-130 cm 43-45 cm 250-280 g Round · foam or soft EVA
9-10 years 128-142 cm 45-45.5 cm 280-300 g Round or soft teardrop · soft EVA

Rule of thumb: if you are torn between two weights, choose the lighter one. A racket that is too light almost never holds development back; one that is too heavy can create wrist habits and overload.

And a fact that often surprises parents: upgrading the racket too soon does not improve performance and does increase shoulder and elbow load. A biomechanical study of the serve with differently sized rackets found that the smaller racket reduced joint loads without worsening the result.

7.2 Length, shape and balance

For beginners, round shape and low balance (weight towards the handle). That gives a large sweet spot and forgives misses. Diamond-shaped, head-heavy rackets are designed for power: they demand arm strength that a 5-year-old does not have.

To check length, use the floor test: standing up, with the racket head down beside the leg, the handle should reach the palm of the relaxed hand. If the child has to lean over or stretch their arm, the racket is too long.

7.3 The grip

The child's hand should close comfortably. Home test: an adult's index finger should fit with slight resistance between the child's fingers and palm. If it slides in without touching anything, the grip is too big; if it does not fit, it is too small. A badly sized grip forces compensation with the wrist and is a silent source of elbow and forearm discomfort.

7.4 Material and core

To start: fibreglass and soft EVA or foam. Hard carbon is designed for players with technique and strength; in a child it transmits more vibration and forgives less. There is no need to spend on the "grown-up" racket: at this age, the racket is a learning tool, not a status object.

7.5 Two tests you can do at home

The 30-second test (weight)

The child holds the racket with their arm extended and a neutral wrist for 30 seconds. If the racket head drops, if the wrist bends backwards or if they switch hands, the racket is too heavy.

The 10-swing test (fatigue)

Ask for 10 air swings in a row. If by the end the elbow has drifted away, the shoulder is rising or the racket face is opening, there is fatigue: the racket is too much for their strength.

7.6 When to change racket

  • When they fail the 30-second test from below: the racket is too much.
  • When their height moves into a new band, not when their birthday does.
  • When they play more than once a week and the racket is short on sweet spot, not on weight.
  • Never "so it lasts two years". A big racket bought to grow into is a racket the child drags around for two years.

7.7 The six most common mistakes

  1. A "light" adult racket (350 g). It is not light for a 6-year-old: it is 40% heavier than their racket.
  2. A diamond racket because it is their idol's. The child has neither the strength nor the point of impact to use it.
  3. A standard-pressure ball. It is the most expensive mistake and the easiest to fix: the ball arrives sooner, bounces higher and the child is late to everything.
  4. An adult grip. The hand does not close and the stroke becomes a wrist flick.
  5. Buying by age instead of height. Two 6-year-olds can be 15 cm apart.
  6. Forgetting the wrist cord. In padel it is mandatory and, for children, it is safety.

Parents' frequently asked questions

At what age can a child start playing padel?
From 4 or 5 years old, always with adapted equipment and in a pre-sport format. The rules of padel do not set a minimum age: what matters is that the child can hold a light racket and enjoy a ball game. Before age 4, free play and general movement skills are the priority.
Which padel racket should I buy for a 4, 5 or 6 year old?
A racket around 43 cm long, weighing 200 to 250 grams, round in shape, with a foam or soft EVA core and a small grip. If you are torn between two weights, choose the lighter one. Try the 30-second test: if the racket head drops or the wrist bends backwards, it is too heavy.
Is my child too young? Should I wait until age 8?
No, as long as the class is pre-sport and not early specialisation. Between 4 and 6, children build the movement repertoire that later allows them to learn technique. Early specialisation is linked to more injuries and more drop-out, not to better sporting results.
How many children are in each class and how long does it last?
Between 5 and 8 children per court, in 45-minute sessions once a week. The short format keeps attention and intensity high from start to finish and lets every child rotate through every station.
Do I need to buy a racket or equipment before starting?
No. In Pre-Padel we provide adapted rackets and movement equipment. When the time comes to buy their own racket, I will advise you free of charge on weight, length, shape and grip size.

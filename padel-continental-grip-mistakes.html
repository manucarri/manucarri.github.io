The Padel Grip: The "invisible mistake" preventing you from leveling up

Hi, I'm Manu. When I'm competing on the backhand side, squeezing the most out of my racket and giving the exact power to the ball depends 100% on a millimeter detail: how I place my hand.

The grip is one of the first things I look at when I start giving padel lessons in Valencia to a new student. It's a detail that seems harmless, but it dictates your entire game. If you've been playing with your friends for years, you've spent a fortune on rackets and feel that you are completely stuck at an intermediate level, I assure you the problem is not your equipment. The problem, almost certainly, is in your hand.

"The way you hold the racket conditions the way you hit the ball." — Manu Carrilero

The blank canvas vs. Acquired bad habits

The other day something great happened with a new student. She had played padel barely four times in her life and came to take lessons. For me, as a coach, that is the perfect scenario. We start from scratch, there are no acquired bad habits, and all I have to do is lay the foundation so she can start flying on the court.

However, she brought one of the most typical beginner mistakes: she held the racket too high up, almost choking it, as if it were a table tennis paddle. Other students I find sticking their index finger through the heart of the racket (a brutal danger for the hand tendons) or with the racket turned almost 90 degrees, imitating a tennis "western" grip.

As soon as I realize and see strange shots on the court, I look at the hand, and there's the culprit.

The continental grip: Your life insurance

To solve this, we always go back to the origin.

"Let's say that in padel the base grip is the continental, which is as if we held a hammer with the racket to drive a nail with the frame." — Manu Carrilero

Although at more advanced levels we do play with small changes (switching to "eastern" forehand or backhand grips to boost topspin smashes or adjust volleys), 90% of your amateur game must be supported on the continental grip. It allows you to defend, attack, hit wall exits, and volley without losing fractions of a second changing your hand position.

Why is it so hard to change the grip if you already have a good level?

Here enters the drama of the "stuck player". I'll give you my father-in-law's example. He's been playing padel all his life, and he's always done it with the grip turned a little bit, in what would be an "eastern backhand".

Of course, that conditions all his shots. Right now, it would be torture for him to change that gesture. In fact, he already tried once with another coach and gave up. Why?

"Because it forces you to change your entire technique, all your positioning, and everything you've been doing, since the racket is no longer in the position it used to be." — Manu Carrilero

That is the price to pay. Modifying a wrong grip implies taking two steps back to be able to take ten forward. At first, you will miss balls that you used to put in, you will feel like you have no strength, and you will get frustrated. But if you don't go through that "desert", you will remain limited forever (and besides, you will buy many lottery tickets to suffer from epicondylitis or shoulder pain from forcing the joint into unnatural angles).

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the correct grip to play padel?
The base and most recommended grip for 90% of padel shots is the "continental". It's similar to holding a hammer.
Should I change grips during the match?
At beginner and intermediate levels (up to level 4.0), the ideal is to maintain the continental grip for everything. At advanced levels, micro-adjustments are made to optimize kicksmashes (por tres) or very aggressive volleys.
Can a bad grip cause injuries?
Yes. Holding the racket incorrectly forces the wrist, elbow, and shoulder to compensate for the impact angle, being one of the main causes of epicondylitis (tennis elbow) and joint overloads.

Why I NEVER teach the "boast" (contrapared) to my beginner padel students

Hi, I'm Manu. Padel has something magical that differentiates it from tennis: the walls. The walls are not the limit of the court; they are your best allies. Learning to play with them is one of the most fun processes in this sport.

However, to play well with the walls, you have to know how to use them with your head. And this is where I see the biggest disaster on club courts, both in beginner players and in supposedly more advanced profiles (those who have been playing for years but feel they aren't improving).

Today I'm going to tell you why in my padel lessons in Valencia I have "forbidden" teaching the boast (contra-pared) in the early stages.

The problem with overusing the boast: The laziness trap

We tend to overuse the boast. We see it in professional tournaments, and it seems like a spectacular resource. But, in amateur padel, reality is very different.

"Overusing the boast is a way of wanting to be more innovative sometimes, of wanting to run less, of wanting to position yourself worse, and being a bit lazier. And that is the main problem." — Manu Carrilero

When you trust that the back wall is going to save you, you stop doing the most important thing in padel: moving your legs, getting to the ball earlier, bending down, and turning sideways. You substitute physical effort and the technical work of a good wall exit for an emergency resource that, poorly executed, is a gift to your opponents.

"If you learn to use it every time you have the chance, in the end, you are going to sell yourself short, you are going to miss a lot of balls, and it's not the best idea." — Manu Carrilero

My wife's case: From zero to competing without hitting a single boast

I'm not just talking to talk. I'll give you my wife's example. She's been playing padel for two years; I've been teaching her in classes and playing matches with her. Sometimes she jokingly complains that I still haven't taught her how to hit a boast.

But the results speak for themselves: in just two years, she has gone from never having touched a racket in her life to playing level 3.5 matches. Something tells me we are doing things right.

And it's not the only case; it's what happens with students who start from the beginner level without acquired bad habits. By not having the "escape route" of the boast, their brain and physique are forced to:

  1. Learn to position themselves well on the court.
  2. Not get lazy when going towards the wall.
  3. Learn to stand sideways correctly.
  4. Get to the ball sooner to take advantage of a textbook wall exit.

And what about the side boasts?

The side wall boast is almost a happy accident. You learn it, quote-unquote, by bouncing around and joking on the court. It is not a tactical resource upon which you should build your defense when you are learning.

In my classes, I apply one of the principles of the MM Academy methodology: technique must be at the service of tactics. For this reason, I prefer my students to build a solid foundation, to understand how to anticipate the ball.

When you master all that, I will teach you how to hit a boast to draw applause. Until then: bend your knees, turn sideways, and leave laziness on the bench.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a boast (contra-pared) in padel?
It is a defensive shot in which the player hits the ball against the glass of their own court so that, after the rebound, it passes over the net into the opposing court.
When should I use the boast?
It should only be used as a last resort emergency when you have been completely beaten and it is physically impossible to position yourself behind the ball to make a conventional wall exit.
Why do I fail so much when trying to hit the ball off the glass?
Usually, it fails due to a lack of positioning, hitting the ball too low, or not using the power of the legs to follow through with the lob, overusing just the arm.

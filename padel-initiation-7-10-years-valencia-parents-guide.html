Padel initiation (7-10 years): what we coach at 7-8, what changes at 9-10 and how to choose the racket

By Manu Carrilero · BSc in Sport Sciences (CAFYD) and Padel Coach in Valencia

If your child is between 7 and 10 years old, you are at the age where most children arrive in padel. And it is no coincidence. For years, the child of that age who wanted a racket went to tennis, and the one who did not ended up in swimming, football or gymnastics. Padel grew later, and today it is one of the first options when a family looks for a racket sport close to home.

You can see it in my academy: the 7-10 group is the one that grows most every season, and it is also, probably, the best age to start. Not because younger children cannot — Pre-Padel exists and works — but because at 7-10 a child understands an instruction, can repeat it and enjoys the challenge of getting better.

But there is a detail almost nobody mentions, and it changes everything: two different children live inside that group. The 7-8 year old, in the best coordination moment of their sporting life. And the 9-10 year old, who already wants to direct the ball, play with a partner and compete. Coaching both the same way is the most common mistake in padel academies.

In this article I explain what we coach in each sub-block, why a growth spurt can throw coordination off, what a real 45-minute session looks like, when competition makes sense and how to choose the racket without overloading the wrist.

Quick answer for parents in a hurry

  • Age: 7 to 10. It is the most common entry range in padel.
  • Goal: a stable point of impact, global strokes (forehand, backhand, volley) and moving before hitting.
  • Format: one 45-minute session per week, 4-6 children per court, 100% indoor court.
  • Equipment: a 250-280 g racket at 7-8 and 280-300 g at 9-10; red or orange ball depending on the phase.
  • Fee: 55 €/month, 0 € registration, technical equipment included.
  • Good sign: they repeat the stroke without thinking and want to come back next week.

1. What Initiation is and how it differs from Pre-Padel

The difference fits in one sentence: Pre-Padel builds the body; Initiation builds the stroke. In the 4-6 group the racket is a friendly object and the goal is for the child to move well. Here the racket becomes a tool, and the goal is for the child to learn to use it with judgement.

1.1 From body to stroke: the change of goal

At 7-8 something appears that did not exist at 5: the ability to repeat a movement on purpose. The child distinguishes left from right, can imitate a model with precision and accepts a correction without losing the smile. That is why we do teach technique here, but in a global format: the general shape of the stroke, not the millimetre detail.

There is a rule I repeat in every session: one correction per class, and better as an image ("the racket looks at the sky", "push the wall") than as a technical concept. An 8-year-old does not need to know what pronation is; they need to hit two hundred times with a ball that gives them time.

1.2 The age families arrive in padel

Historically, the 7-10 year old who started a racket sport did it in tennis, and the one looking for a base sport ended up in swimming, athletics or football. Padel joined that conversation much later. Today the picture has changed: it is rare to find a weekend in Valencia without a court full of children of this age.

The cover image of this article is from 2013, in La Pobla de Vallbona: a clay court, a net, balls everywhere and a whole class enjoying themselves. More than a decade later, what I see in Paterna is the same, with an indoor court and more families than ever. This is the natural entry age for padel, and it is worth doing well.

1.3 What we do and what we do not

  • Yes: global forehand, backhand and volley technique, with adapted equipment and plenty of useful repetitions.
  • Yes: moving before hitting (split-step, lateral movement, recovery).
  • Yes: cooperative play, challenges and first rules. At this age you learn by playing, not by waiting.
  • No: power. Power is a consequence of technique and maturation, not a goal at 8.
  • No: complex tactics (double walls, counter-attacks, patterns). The perceptual base is not there yet.
  • No: specialisation. The child should keep doing other sports and free play.

2. 7-8 years: the golden age of coordination

2.1 What the science says about this band

Between 7 and 8, sustained attention rises to 10-15 minutes, the child distinguishes left and right and loves to repeat in order to master something. It is the stage where the nervous system automates patterns more easily than at any other time in a sporting life. Coordination, rhythm and spatial orientation respond like at no other age.

It is worth being honest about what science says and what it does not. The classic model spoke of "windows" that close; Van Hooren and De Ste Croix (2020) reviewed that idea and concluded there is no evidence for generic windows per ability. What does hold is simpler and more useful: this is the stage where the same work pays off most. It is not a door closing, it is a slope in your favour.

2.2 The golden age is not a straight line: when the growth spurt disrupts it

There is a nuance you can see clearly on court and that almost no article mentions. Around 6 years old many children go through a sweet spot: they are spontaneous, they move easily and, because the body has not had its growth spurt yet, coordination improves at an astonishing rate. It is their best version in terms of body control.

From then on, and especially in some children, growth speeds up. Long bones grow before the nervous system has recalculated the distances: the arm is longer, so are the legs, and the child who hit cleanly three months ago suddenly arrives late, gets tangled or loses range of movement. They have not got worse: their body has changed size. The growth spurt that disrupts most is the pubertal one, around 11-14, but growth peaks at these ages are also very noticeable.

I lived it first-hand. I grew in a hurry and, for a while, my body did not do what I asked of it. And I have seen it many times from the outside. In 2012 I worked with a boy, the son of a padel coach, who played beautifully: he had great hands and had carried a racket since he was tiny. He grew suddenly, lost coordination and his body stopped living up to what he imagined on court. We worked together for about a year, patiently and without obsessing over precision, and he improved enormously. Today, as far as I know, he lives from padel: he had a professional career, won titles and now coaches abroad.

What do we do when that dip appears?

  1. Do not correct more: accompany. The child does not need to hear that they are getting worse; they need to keep hitting.
  2. Lower the precision demand and keep technique at submaximal intensity. It is not the time for records.
  3. Work on mobility of ankle, hip and chest, and on landing control. That is what stops the dip becoming an injury.
  4. Measure height every term to know whether the change is growth or something else.
  5. Do not compare with the team-mate who has not had their spurt yet. The difference is calendar, not talent.

That is why we group by development and not only by year of birth, and why the 4-6 children per court ratio matters: at these ages, one centimetre more or less changes the way they hit.

2.3 Point of impact: the movement that changes everything

If I had to choose one thing from the 7-8 years, it would be this: always hit in front of the body and at hip height. It is not cosmetic. When the point of impact is in front, the child hits with the body aligned, sees the ball and can direct it. When it is beside or behind, they compensate with the wrist, lose direction and end up with elbow and forearm discomfort.

The fastest way to build that point of impact is with a suspended ball: a ball hanging from a string at the right height. The child hits, sees where they make contact and repeats fifty times without the ball escaping. It is the most boring drill in the world for an adult and the most effective one for an 8-year-old.

2.4 Adapted court and ball: red and orange

The expert-validated model for youth padel proposes three practice environments: red court (10 × 6 m, low net, red ball), orange (16 × 8 m, intermediate net, orange ball) and green (regulation court with a green ball). The key is not to invent a different mini-padel, but to keep the essence —2v2, net, walls, cooperation— and adapt the size.

And the fact that matters most: in tennis, with a smaller court and a slower ball, children hit more times per session and learn more (Farrow & Reid, 2010). The same happens in padel. More useful repetitions is not a method, it is arithmetic.

3. 9-10 years: consolidating the stroke and starting to read the game

3.1 From repeating the movement to directing the ball

Around 9-10 attention rises to 15-20 minutes, the child understands cause and effect and can self-correct with a cue. Something new appears: they want to compete and they want to belong to the group. It is the moment to introduce rules, roles and small challenges, and to ask for something that made no sense before: that the ball goes where they decide.

Here the goal stops being "hitting well" and becomes "hitting well to a place". First cross-court and down the line with targets, then with opposition. Without this direction, the child accumulates strokes with no intention and stalls as soon as the opponent's level rises.

3.2 The wall: the first quality leap

The wall is what makes padel padel, and at 9-10 it can already be trained properly. The progression is always the same: throw with the hand against the glass and catch after the bounce, several times; then with the racket; then read, position and hit. First one wall, later two.

The idea is not to teach the boast —that comes later and with judgement— but for the child to learn to read the ball coming out of the wall. It is the prerequisite for everything that follows.

3.3 First decisions and playing as a pair

Complex tactics mature late, but simple decisions can be trained at 9-10 with rules: "you can only attack if the ball bounces before the line", "if you go up, your partner covers". The rule teaches the decision without explaining it, and the child discovers it by playing.

Real pair play also starts: I go up, you cover, communicate, do not collide. At this age we are not looking for a stable partner or results; we are looking for the child to learn that in padel there is always someone beside them.

4. The five blocks we work on court

Every session touches the five blocks, in a different order and with different games. They are not subjects: they are the pieces of the stroke that later combine.

4.1 Basic strokes: forehand, backhand and volley

Forehand and backhand are built on the same principle: short preparation, impact in front, long follow-through. The volley is introduced earlier than it seems, because it is the stroke closest to what the child does at break time: block and push. No power; direction first.

4.2 Controlling the racket face

Before hitting hard you have to know where the racket is looking. We work with the racket still and the ball thrown on purpose: the child must hit and see whether the ball goes straight, cross-court or high. It is the same principle as using a target, but without a target: the racket face decides.

4.3 Movement: split-step and recovery

Padel is played with the legs. We teach the split-step (that small hop before the opponent hits) and recovery to the centre after every shot. At first it looks like a strange dance; a few weeks later it is automatic and the child reaches balls they did not reach before.

4.4 Rhythm and tempo

Hitting well is hitting on time. We work with claps, with balls bouncing to a rhythm and with preparation-hit-finish sequences counted out loud. A child with good tempo seems to play more slowly, and in fact arrives earlier.

4.5 First wall bounce and reading

As a game, not as technique: throw against the glass, guess where the ball will come out and get into position. It is the block that shows most at 10 and the one that separates the child who "hits well" from the one who starts to "play".

5. Concrete drills you will see in a session

These are some of the ones I repeat most in the 7-10 group, with what each one trains and why it works:

6. What a 45-minute session looks like

The structure changes little, because routine gives security and lets the child anticipate what comes next:

  • 0-8 minutes · Ball warm-up. A movement game with a ball: moving, bouncing, passing. It helps them warm up and leave the backpack outside.
  • 8-28 minutes · Technical block. Two strokes maximum per session (forehand and volley, for example), with stations and many repetitions. One correction, one image.
  • 28-40 minutes · Conditioned play. Cooperative rally or points with one rule. Here everything comes together and the child decides.
  • 40-45 minutes · Closing. A short challenge, one question ("what did you get right today?") and home with a concrete win.

Why 45 minutes and not an hour? Because an 8-year-old's attention runs out before an adult's. In forty-five minutes intensity stays high from start to finish, without the last ten minutes of fatigue and distraction that ruin motivation. Adults train 60; children, 45.

Why 4-6 children per court? Because at this age correction has to be individual and frequent. With more children, the coach becomes a steward. With fewer, you lose the social play that also teaches at this age.

7. The racket at 7-10 years old

The question I get most on WhatsApp. The short answer is the same as at 5: weight, shape and grip, in that order. Length matters less than it seems, because the rules limit it a lot (45.5 cm maximum, FIP).

7.1 Weight and height: the table

Age Approx. height Length Weight Shape and core
7-8 years 115-130 cm 43-45 cm 250-280 g Round · foam or soft EVA
9-10 years 128-142 cm 45-45.5 cm 280-300 g Round or soft teardrop · soft EVA
11-12 years 140-155 cm 45.5 cm 300-320 g Teardrop · medium EVA

Two honesty warnings. First: in padel there is no official junior sizing system like tennis' Play & Stay; shop bands are commercial recommendations and vary between brands. Second: height rules over birthday. Two 9-year-olds can be 15 cm apart, and the racket should follow the body, not the ID card.

7.2 Two tests you can do at home

The 30-second test (weight)

The child holds the racket with their arm extended and a neutral wrist for 30 seconds. If the racket head drops, if the wrist bends backwards or if they switch hands, the racket is too heavy.

The 10-swing test (fatigue)

Ask for 10 air swings in a row. If by the end the elbow has drifted away, the shoulder is rising or the racket face is opening, there is fatigue: the racket is too much for their strength.

7.3 When to change racket and the most common mistakes

  • When their height moves into a new band, not when their birthday does.
  • When they play more than once a week and the racket is short on sweet spot, not on weight.
  • When they fail the 30-second test from below: the racket is too much.
  • Never "so it lasts two years". A big racket bought to grow into is a racket the child drags around for two years.

And the mistakes I see every week:

  1. A "light" adult racket (350 g). It is not light for a 9-year-old: it is 20-30% heavier than theirs.
  2. A diamond racket because it is their idol's. It demands strength and a point of impact that do not exist at this age.
  3. A standard-pressure ball. The most expensive mistake and the easiest to fix: the ball arrives sooner and the child is late to everything.
  4. An adult grip. The hand does not close and the stroke becomes a wrist flick.
  5. Buying by age instead of height. Repeated on purpose, because it is mistake number one.
  6. Forgetting the wrist cord. In padel it is mandatory and, for children, it is safety.

If you want to go deeper into the movement and into why equipment matters so much for the arm, there are two reads: the continental grip and its mistakes and the guide to elbow biomechanics and injuries in padel.

8. Competition: when to start and how

It is the question that arrives early, almost always from the adult and not from the child. Spanish federation categories run, with variations by season and region, from benjamín (8-10) to junior (16-18). The fact that a category exists does not mean competing is the best thing for an 8-year-old.

The criterion I use is pedagogical, not federative:

  • Before 9-10: competition as play. Short formats, no harsh knockout, no ranking. The goal is for them to want to come back.
  • 10-12: first local tournaments, with a consolation draw. We teach them to compete (routine, warm-up, handling mistakes), not to win.
  • 13-16: regular competition if the player wants it, with volume control (maximum 2 tournaments a month) and never replacing training.

Warning sign: the child stops enjoying it, gets nervous before playing or only talks about results. That is where the priority has flipped, and the problem is not the child.

If your child competes, video analysis is the most useful tool from 10-11 onwards: 30 seconds recorded and watched once works as a mirror; full analysis comes later.

9. What changes for parents at this age

Much less than it seems. The sideline rules are the same as at 5 —distance, silence and zero instructions— but a new risk appears: the result.

  • Less sideline, more autonomy. At 9-10 the child can get ready alone: bottle, racket, wrist cord. Let them.
  • The question afterwards stays the same: "did you enjoy it?" and "what did you get right today?". Never "did you win?".
  • The child starts comparing themselves. Do not add the adult's comparison: "look how Johnny plays" does not help.
  • Ask before buying. Before buying, ask me: better to wait and ask than to have to return it or gamble with an injury.
  • If there is a tournament, the goal is taking part. The standings are a consequence, not the target.

Parents' frequently asked questions

At what age can a child start competing in padel?
Between 7 and 9, competition should be play: short formats, no harsh knockout rounds and no ranking. From 9-10 it makes sense to start with local tournaments that include a consolation draw, and always because the child wants to, not because the adult does. Before 12, the goal is learning to compete, not winning.
Which padel racket does a 7, 8, 9 or 10 year old need?
At 7-8, a racket of 43 to 45 cm and 250 to 280 grams. At 9-10, 45 to 45.5 cm and 280 to 300 grams. In both cases: round shape, low balance and a soft core (foam or soft EVA). If you are torn between two weights, choose the lighter one.
Is it too late to start padel at 9 or 10 years old?
No. At 9-10 a child learns technique faster than at 5, because there is more attention, more body control and the ability to understand a correction. What is lost compared with starting earlier is movement repertoire, and that is offset with variety of movement and other sports.
How long does it take to see progress in a child's padel?
With one session a week, the first visible changes in point of impact and positioning appear in 8-12 weeks. Big coordination changes take months. If after three months there is no improvement at all and the child is enjoying it, the problem is usually the dose, not the talent.
Can a child move from Pre-Padel to Initiation mid-season?
Yes, and it is the usual thing. The step is based on age and maturity, not on the term: if the child already holds the racket with control, can pay attention for 10-15 minutes and shows interest in hitting, they are ready. We assess it together in a trial session.
